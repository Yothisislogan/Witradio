## The idea
- Insurance moves the cost of a loss from one person to a group: many people each pay a smaller, known amount so the few who have a loss are paid.
- What each person buys is certainty: a known premium instead of an unknown, possibly ruinous loss.
- The money that pays claims comes mostly from the premiums of the people in the pool who didn't have a loss that year.

## The law of large numbers
- With a handful of policies, losses are a coin toss. With thousands of similar policies, the number of losses becomes predictable.
- That predictability is what lets an insurer set a price ahead of time.
- It only works when the exposures in the pool are similar and independent of each other.

## When a pool breaks
- A catastrophe hits many policyholders at once, so losses are no longer independent: a hurricane or a wildfire is the classic case.
- Insurers manage that by spreading risk geographically and by buying insurance of their own, called reinsurance.
- Adverse selection: if mostly high-risk people buy coverage, the pool's losses outrun its premiums. Underwriting exists to keep the pool balanced.

## What it means for you
- Your premium reflects the pool you're placed in and your own risk characteristics.
- Small losses you could pay yourself are often better kept out of the pool, which is part of why deductibles exist.
